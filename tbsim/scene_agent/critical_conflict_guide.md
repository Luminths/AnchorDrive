# Key Safety Scenario Generation Guide

Based on the current scene image and vehicle states, independently conceive a key safety scenario, and finally use `define_goal` to fix the English-language goal and participating vehicles. The NHTSA categories below are used to inspire dangerous interactions between vehicles; they can be selected and combined according to the current road conditions, and it is not necessary to try each category or to use a fixed category.

## Scenario Generation Method

1. **Conceive from the image first.** Use `view_scene` to observe EGO, the positions of surrounding vehicles, their heading directions, and the road shape. Referring to the NHTSA categories below, before querying vehicle or road details, you must first propose a preliminary idea in 2–4 English sentences: which vehicle or where to spawn a vehicle, what it does, how it forms a conflict with the moving EGO, whether an auxiliary vehicle is needed and its role; state the information to be verified, and do not treat the image description as the idea. Then query only the uncertain information related to that idea; if unsuitable conditions are found, you may modify or abandon the idea, and you do not need to query the entire map first. In the image, EGO is centered and its heading points upward; query coordinates are `[meters ahead of EGO, meters to the left of EGO]`. A vehicle's own left and right must be judged together with its heading direction.
2. **Independently choose the participation method.** You may choose an existing vehicle as the attacking vehicle or auxiliary vehicle, spawn a vehicle, or combine both. All existing non-EGO vehicles Vxxx may be selected; after including them in `define_goal.participants`, in the subsequent execution phase they use the same behavior control capabilities as spawned vehicles Sx, and can change speed, steer, or change lanes. Spawned vehicles have no additional control permissions; choose vehicles according to vehicle states and scenario needs, and do not spawn another vehicle out of concern that an existing vehicle is uncontrollable. At the current stage, only fix the goal and participating vehicles; the actual actions are issued and executed by the subsequent closed-loop Agent. Use `get_vehicles` to verify the speed, position, heading, and lane of relevant vehicles. When using only existing vehicles, it is usually sufficient to conceive the goal from the image and vehicle information, and it is not necessary to query a large amount of road information for each vehicle.
3. **Query roads only when a spawned vehicle is needed.** Select an ordinary lane `Lxxx` from the image, or query a known connection segment `Cxxx`, and use `get_lane` to view the EGO coordinates of the directed centerline and the heading of each point relative to EGO. C is a directed connection path within an intersection, represented in the image by a light-colored curve; when two ordinary lanes are known, you can use `get_lane_connections(from_lane_id, to_lane_id)` to query the C between the entrance and exit and its centerline, without guessing the C number. Coordinates are arranged from the start point to the end point according to the lane's direction of travel; adjacent lanes must not be mistaken for same-direction lanes. Currently `spawn_vehicle` only accepts ordinary L lanes.
4. **Determine the spawn position yourself.** You may specify `distance_along_lane_m` measured from the start point along the lane centerline, or specify EGO coordinates `x_m` (positive ahead) and `y_m` (positive left); choose one of the two forms, and specify the lane ID and initial speed at the same time. The coordinate form keeps the selected position, and the heading is set according to the nearby lane direction; it must be at least 2 meters from both ends of the lane. The underlying layer checks road legality and vehicle body occupancy, and adjustments are made according to feedback if it fails. If after spawning you find that the position or plan is unsuitable, you can use `delete_spawned_vehicle(actor_id=\"S1\")` to delete the vehicle you spawned, and then reselect a position; deletion releases the position and slot, EGO and original vehicles cannot be deleted, and subsequent spawned vehicles use new IDs. When calling `view_scene`, highlight only the attacking vehicle and auxiliary vehicle you intend to control, and do not highlight EGO or irrelevant background vehicles; after selecting vehicles, check their positions, orientations, and nearby traffic.
5. **There may be multiple attacking vehicles, and auxiliary vehicles may also be set as needed.** It is not limited to one main attacking vehicle plus auxiliary vehicles; two or three vehicles may directly attack EGO, with attacking vehicles and auxiliary vehicles totaling at most 3, counting existing vehicles and spawned vehicles together, with EGO not counted, and it is not necessary to fill the quota. For an intersection crossing plan, you may select or spawn one attacking vehicle in each of two suitable adjacent lanes, and coordinate them by speed and departure timing to cross EGO's path, forming simultaneous or staggered convergence windows; first verify lane orientations, legal positions, and background vehicles, since adjacent lanes are not necessarily same-direction. Auxiliary vehicles may occupy another feasible passage or form another conflict, but the specific actions and cooperative roles of each vehicle must be clearly stated; they must not merely follow without effect, and must not collide with each other or pass through background vehicles. The simulation stops at first contact; a multi-attacking-vehicle goal may require a joint attack and at least one of them to collide with EGO, and does not require each vehicle to collide in sequence; the assisting effect must occur before first contact. If space and timing do not support it, use only one vehicle.
6. **Fix a short, qualitative goal.** Roughly describe the attack process of the participating vehicles and the final actual body collision with EGO, for example, "S1 waits stationary in the adjacent lane ahead, then starts and changes lanes to hit EGO when EGO approaches from the side." Preserve this qualitative action relationship, and do not write specific values such as speeds, distances, coordinates, angles, or seconds (except vehicle IDs); leave specific anchors and timing to the closed-loop execution Agent. Write initial-state values in the rationale as needed. A collision threat, small gap, or near miss does not count as achievement; do not write it as a choice between "collision or small gap"; when cooperation is needed, state the order of events. Do not overly restrict whether contact is at the door or rear corner; EGO is controlled by an independent planning and control module, and the Agent can only control attacking vehicles and auxiliary vehicles, not EGO, so do not require EGO to brake, change lanes, accelerate, or drive along a specified route, and do not assume unknown traffic light states or the vehicle's future route. Direct rear-end collision with EGO from directly behind in the same direction and same lane is prohibited; side or rear-corner contact produced by diagonal cutting from an adjacent lane is allowed. If information is insufficient, query the relevant tools again; if there is enough basis, submit one goal.
7. **Background collisions cannot replace the target collision.** Collisions with background vehicles are allowed; background collisions do not stop the simulation and do not by themselves make the goal fail; the specified attacking vehicle must still actually collide with EGO. Consider whether background vehicles block the interaction passage, and if necessary adjust the participating vehicles, positions, or mechanism; do not misreport background contact as EGO contact.

8. **The conflict occurs in the future, so consider EGO's own motion.** Do not treat EGO's current position in the image as the position where it will remain. Combine EGO's current speed, heading direction, and road alignment to estimate where it will move while the attacking vehicle starts, accelerates, turns, or changes lanes, and roughly compare the times for both sides to reach the same conflict area. Roads being connected or paths intersecting does not mean the two vehicles will arrive at the same time; if EGO is likely to have already passed when the attacking vehicle arrives, adjust the action, position, or participating vehicles. EGO may also decelerate or change route, so the estimate under the current speed is only a reference, and it must not be assumed that it will definitely drive as predicted or wait for the attacking vehicle.

9. **In congestion, prioritize lane-change collisions.** The initial position of an attacking vehicle for a lane-change collision should be ahead of EGO (EGO coordinate x_m > 0, including left-front or right-front), for both existing vehicles and spawned vehicles, and do not adopt a plan of catching up from behind and then cutting in. When traffic flow is dense, vehicles are queued, or spacing is small, prioritize conceiving that an opposing vehicle near EGO starts from an adjacent lane or adjusts speed, then changes lanes toward EGO's travel path, cuts diagonally, and collides. First check whether nearby existing vehicles are suitable; do not pass through other background vehicles in order to cut in. If lateral space or interaction timing does not support it, then consider other mechanisms, and do not force a lane-change scenario.
10. **A stationary vehicle starting to attack may also be conceived.** The initial position of this type of attacking vehicle should also be ahead of EGO (x_m > 0, including left-front or right-front), starting from a stationary position ahead to intrude into EGO's path, not starting from behind to chase and hit. In ordinary scenarios, being currently stationary does not mean it cannot participate in an attack. You may choose an existing stationary vehicle, or spawn a vehicle with zero initial speed at a legal position that does not overlap with vehicles, and let it start from rest, accelerate, and turn to intrude into EGO's travel path, forming a collision. It is necessary to consider the time required for starting, accelerating, and lateral movement, and where EGO will advance during that time; you must not merely arrange for a vehicle to remain stationary in place waiting for a collision, and you must not assume that EGO will stop and wait for it. Direct rear-end collision with EGO from directly behind in the same direction and same lane is still prohibited.
11. **Prioritize the lateral collision timing when approaching side by side.** Do not construct a plan in which the attacking vehicle first cuts directly in front of EGO and then decelerates or stops, waiting for EGO to rear-end the attacking vehicle; EGO can generally avoid it by braking. An attacking vehicle in the adjacent lane ahead may first remain in the adjacent lane, decelerate, or adjust speed to wait for EGO to approach, and then at the moment it is about to be side by side or at another suitable time, change lanes, intrude laterally, and collide; a stationary vehicle in the adjacent lane ahead should also start and intrude laterally in coordination with EGO's approach timing, rather than first driving in front of EGO and then stopping. Determine the action start point by combining the relative positions, speeds, and the time required for starting and lateral movement of both sides, and do not mechanically wait until they are completely side by side before turning. The actual timing is adjusted by the execution Agent based on observations, and EGO must not be required to wait or maintain a specified speed.

### NHTSA Pre-Crash Vehicle Relationships: Categories 16–31

The numbers and English names come from the NHTSA report "[Pre-Crash Scenario Typology for Crash Avoidance Research](https://www.nhtsa.gov/sites/nhtsa.gov/files/pre-crash_scenario_typology-final_pdf_version_5-2-07.pdf)" (2007). The original report categorizes pre-crash vehicle motion relationships; the English explanations below are used to help conceive scenarios and must not be used to conclude that a certain action will necessarily cause a collision.

| Number | Original Category | Referenceable Vehicle Relationship |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 16 | Vehicle(s) Turning – Same Direction | Same-direction vehicles whose paths come close while turning, for example competing for the same turning exit. |
| 17 | Vehicle(s) Parking – Same Direction | Parking-related maneuvers in same-direction traffic, for example a vehicle entering or leaving a roadside position. Borrow this only when the current road actually has the corresponding space, and do not assume parking spaces out of thin air. |
| 18 | Vehicle(s) Changing Lanes – Same Direction | Same-direction vehicles changing lanes or merging into another vehicle's travel space. The common scenario of an adjacent-lane vehicle cutting toward EGO belongs to this category. |
| 19 | Vehicle(s) Drifting – Same Direction | Same-direction vehicles deviating from their original travel position and gradually intruding into an adjacent vehicle's space; lateral offset may be described, without inferring causes such as distraction or loss of control. |
| 20 | Vehicle(s) Making a Maneuver – Opposite Direction | Opposite-direction vehicles entering another vehicle's path through maneuvers such as turning or crossing the line. |
| 21 | Vehicle(s) Not Making a Maneuver – Opposite Direction | Path conflict occurs when opposite-direction vehicles do not perform the above maneuvers. Two opposite parallel lanes being close does not by itself constitute a conflict. |
| 22 | Following Vehicle Making a Maneuver | A following vehicle conflicts with the vehicle ahead while performing a maneuver, for example the following vehicle moves laterally and then intrudes into the leading vehicle's space. This project does not adopt direct rear-end collision with EGO from directly behind in the same direction and same lane. |
| 23 | Lead Vehicle Accelerating | A front-rear vehicle conflict that occurs while the lead vehicle is accelerating. The category only describes the motion state at the time and does not mean that the lead vehicle's acceleration itself will cause a collision. |
| 24 | Lead Vehicle Moving at Lower Constant Speed | The lead vehicle travels at a lower constant speed, and there is a speed difference between the front and rear vehicles. |
| 25 | Lead Vehicle Decelerating | The lead vehicle decelerates, and the gap between the following vehicle and the lead vehicle may shrink. |
| 26 | Lead Vehicle Stopped | The lead vehicle is stopped, and the following vehicle approaches it. One may conceive the process in which the attacking vehicle first enters the relevant path and then stops. |
| 27 | Left Turn Across Path From Opposite Directions at Signalized Junctions | At a signalized intersection, an opposite-direction left-turning vehicle crosses another vehicle's path. When the signal state is unknown, borrow only the crossing relationship of the opposite-direction left turn. |
| 28 | Vehicle Turning Right at Signalized Junctions | At a signalized intersection, a right-turning vehicle conflicts with other traffic. When there is no signal information, do not claim running the light or having the right of way. |
| 29 | Left Turn Across Path From Opposite Directions at Non-Signalized Junctions | At a non-signalized intersection, an opposite-direction left-turning vehicle crosses another vehicle's path. When the control method is unknown, describe only the observable turning and crossing relationship. |
| 30 | Straight Crossing Paths at Non-Signalized Junctions | Vehicles from different directions travel straight through the intersection, and their paths cross; when conceiving, the positions and timing of the two vehicles' arrival must also be considered. |
| 31 | Vehicle(s) Turning at Non-Signalized Junctions | At a non-signalized intersection, at least one vehicle turns and conflicts with another vehicle. Choose the turning direction and exit according to the actual road. |
